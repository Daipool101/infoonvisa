One Schengen visa lets you travel across all 29 countries in the area — but you don't get to choose freely which one issues it. There's a rule, embassies apply it strictly, and applying to the wrong country is a common reason a file gets rejected before anyone even looks at your documents.

If you're new to how Schengen works at all, start with [Schengen visa explained](/blog/schengen-visa-explained). This piece answers the next question: **where do I actually apply?**

## Rule 1 — your main destination

You apply to the country that is your **main destination**: where you'll spend the **most nights**.

> Flying into Frankfurt, then 2 nights in Germany and 6 nights in Spain? **Spain** is your main destination. You apply to the Spanish consulate — not the German one, even though you land in Germany first.

Nights, not days. Not where you fly into, not where your airline is from, not where you found an appointment first.

## Rule 2 — if the nights are equal, the country of first entry decides

When no single country stands out — say 3 nights in Italy, 3 in Austria, 3 in Slovenia — you apply to the country you will **enter the Schengen area through first**.

> 3 nights each in France, Belgium and the Netherlands, arriving into Paris? Equal nights, so **first entry** decides: apply to **France**.

"First entry" means the country where you'll physically clear Schengen immigration, which is your first Schengen landing — including if it's only a connection where you pass through passport control.

## Rule 3 — a single destination is simply that country

Visiting one country only? Apply to that country. This is most applications, and it's the easy case.

## What counts as your "main destination"

Two things trip people up:

**Transit doesn't count as a destination.** If you change planes in Amsterdam without leaving the airport and spend your whole trip in Portugal, Portugal is the main destination. Airside transit isn't a stay.

**Purpose can override nights.** If you're attending a conference in Belgium for two days and then holidaying in France for a week, some consulates will treat **Belgium** as the main destination because it's the *purpose* of the trip. If your trip has a clear reason attached to one country — a business meeting, a wedding, a medical appointment — expect that country to be treated as the main destination even when another has more nights. When it's genuinely ambiguous, ask the consulate rather than guessing.

## Why you can't just pick the easiest consulate

You'll see advice suggesting you apply through whichever country has the shortest appointment queue or the friendliest reputation. Don't.

**Consulates check your itinerary against your application.** Your flight bookings, hotel reservations and travel dates all show where you're actually going. If you apply to Country A while your documents plainly show two nights there and nine in Country B, the file is inconsistent on its face. Likely outcomes:

- **Rejected for applying to the wrong consulate**, with the fee not refunded
- **Refused for an unclear travel purpose** — which is a refusal you must then declare on every future application
- At best, told to reapply at the correct consulate, having lost weeks

And the "reputation" angle is largely a myth. All Schengen states apply the same visa code and the same evidence standards. There is no shortcut country.

## What happens after it's issued

Once you have the visa, the main-destination rule stops mattering for travel. A Schengen visa issued by any member state lets you move freely through the whole area, and you're not obliged to enter through the issuing country.

Two things still apply:

- **Your itinerary should broadly match what you applied with.** Plans change and that's normal, but a visa obtained for a Spain trip that you never visit at all can raise questions on a later application.
- **The 90/180 rule counts across the whole area**, not per country. Your total stay across all 29 countries can't exceed 90 days in any rolling 180-day period.

## A quick way to decide

1. **List your nights per country.**
2. **One country clearly highest?** That's where you apply.
3. **Tied?** Apply to your first Schengen entry point.
4. **Does one country carry the trip's purpose** — work, an event, family? Expect that one to count as the main destination.
5. **Then check the practicalities:** many countries outsource applications to a visa centre, so find the correct centre for that country in your city.

## Before you book an appointment

Have your **nights per country settled first**. Appointment slots can be scarce, and rebooking at a different consulate after you realise you chose wrong can cost you the trip.

Then check the requirements for your specific route — documents and processing times differ by nationality even within Schengen. You can look up your own corridor on our [country guides](/countries), and it's worth reading [how early to apply for a visa](/blog/how-early-to-apply-for-a-visa) before you fix your dates.

*Consulates set their own submission procedures and may interpret the main-destination rule slightly differently in borderline cases. Always confirm on the official consulate or visa-centre website for the country you're applying to.*
